Workplace violence against healthcare workers

Injuries from intentional violence by another person, per 10,000 full-time workers, by U.S. healthcare facility type, 2011 to 2021/2022. Pick a year for the big picture, then click any facility to drill down year by year.

What counts as a violent event?

Definitions differ by source. The numbers on this page use the narrowest one: the first definition below.

Counted in this data

The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts a case when an employer records a work injury caused by "intentional injury by other person" (event code 111). It's counted only if the worker missed at least one day of work.

Examples include hitting, kicking, beating or shoving; stabbing or cutting; shooting; strangulation; rape or sexual assault; and threats or verbal assault that led to an injury or illness.

Source: BLS injury classification manual (OIICS)

Workplace violence

OSHA: "any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation, or other threatening disruptive behavior that occurs at the work site."

NIOSH: "violent acts (including physical assaults and threats of assaults) directed toward persons at work or on duty."

Sources: OSHA, as quoted in Lombardi et al. 2024; NIOSH, as quoted in Joint Commission Sentinel Event Alert 59 (2018)

Safety event and sentinel event

Hospitals report incidents as safety events. The most serious are sentinel events: a patient safety event that results in death, permanent harm, or severe temporary harm.

The Joint Commission counts "rape, assault (leading to death, permanent harm, or severe temporary harm), or homicide of a patient, staff member, licensed independent practitioner, visitor, or vendor while on site" as a sentinel event.

Source: Joint Commission Sentinel Event Alert 59, April 2018

Why it matters: verbal abuse, threats and assaults that cause no missed workdays are not in these numbers. Read these rates as the most serious cases only; worker surveys report far more violence than employer injury records show.

About the data

  • Source: Lombardi B, Jensen T, Galloway E, Fraher E. "Trends in workplace violence for health care occupations and facilities over the last 10 years." Health Affairs Scholar. 2024;2(12):qxae134, Table 1. doi.org/10.1093/haschl/qxae134. The authors built it from the Bureau of Labor Statistics Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses.
  • Measure: injuries with at least one day away from work caused by an intentional injury by another person, per 10,000 full-time workers. Every number on this page uses this same measure, including the headline comparison with all private industry (BLS fact sheet, 2018).
  • Change compares 3-year averages (2011-2013 vs. 2018-2020, the latest three single years) instead of single years, so one unusual year can't drive the result. 2021/22 is a two-year period, so it is shown but not used for change. 2020 was a pandemic year; using 2017-2019 instead still shows most facility types rising (9 of 12).

Known limits and possible bias

These can't be removed from the data, so read the numbers with them in mind.

  • Undercounting: cases come from employer injury records. Violence that is never reported, or that causes no missed workday (most verbal abuse and threats), is not counted. Reporting also differs among staff; younger nurses are more likely to report (Friese et al., 2024). True rates are higher, and the gap may not be the same in every setting.
  • Reporting culture can look like a trend: a rising rate can mean more violence, better reporting, or both. This data can't tell them apart.
  • Missing facility types: six types with blank years in Table 1 are left out, so no value is guessed: offices of physicians, offices of mental health physicians, offices of other health practitioners, medical and diagnostic laboratories, community food and housing services, and child day care. Totals and counts on this page cover only the 12 shown.
  • Private employers only: the BLS tables behind these rates cover private industry, so government-run hospitals may not be included.
  • Small numbers swing: rates for smaller industries move a lot from year to year. Use averages and multi-year change, not a single year.
  • No information about who caused the harm: the data describes injured workers and settings only. High rates in psychiatric and residential settings reflect the intensity of care in those settings. They should not be read as a statement about people with mental illness or disabilities in general.
  • Serious event analyses are confidential: data from incident reporting systems related to Serious Event analysis cannot be obtained or shared here. What happened in an event may be shared; the analysis of what led to it (root cause and contributing-factor findings) may not. This page uses only public, published data.
